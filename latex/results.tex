The UNSW-NB15 results expose a dataset-level limitation not present in CIC-IoT2023. The LLM achieves a macro F1 of only $0.07$ versus Random Forest's $0.63$, a 9$\times$ gap compared to the 2$\times$ gap observed on CIC-IoT2023 ($0.45$ vs.\ $0.89$). We observe the gap is not solely an LLM weakness: even Random Forest struggles on Analysis~(0.32), Backdoor~(0.24), and DoS~(0.27), indicating overlapping traffic signatures at the feature level. The root cause is convergence: multiple attack classes independently learn near-identical volume based rules causing the proportion-of-rules fired scorer to tie across classes and default to the Normal label for the majority of test rows. This is apparent in the high precision but near-zero recall for Generic~(1.00/0.02) and Reconnaissance~(1.00/0.007). Hence, the rules are correct when they fire, but fire so rarely that $>$90\% of true instances are missed.
The results reveal that UNSW-NB15's sub-class attack
families are statistically nearly indistinguishable in the feature space sampled, and the zero-shot rule-generation approach cannot resolve this ambiguity without gradient-based training on the discriminative directions between classes.

Binary classification baselines across the remaining three datasets are included in Table~\ref{tab:anon_ablation}, which reports both original and anonymized F1 for all five datasets. These serve as the basis for the anonymization and zero-day analyses in subsequent sections. WUSTL-IIoT, TON\_IoT, and Bot-IoT are evaluated in binary mode rather than multi-class mode for the following reasons: WUSTL-IIoT has only four classes, making the multi-class and binary settings nearly equivalent in scope; Bot-IoT's corpus is severely size-limited (to preserve class balance) at 740 training samples, which is insufficient for stable per-class rule generation across five attack families; and TON\_IoT's ten attack families exhibit substantial traffic-signature overlap at the flow level, making it a stronger subject for the zero-day and anonymization experiments than for multi-class classification. The multi-class setting is therefore focused on CIC-IoT2023 and UNSW-NB15, which respectively represent the best-case (high inter-class separability)
and worst-case (high intra-class overlap) outcomes for the method.

\subsection{Zero-Day Detection Analysis}
\label{subsec:zeroday_results}
%=======================================================================

Table~\ref{tab:zeroday_summary} reports zero-day detection performance for
each dataset. The ZDR (Zero-Day Detection Rate) column denotes the fraction of withheld-class flows correctly flagged as anomalous; Known-F1 is the macro-averaged F1 on the remaining known classes. A ZDR near 1.0 indicates that the LLM-generated rules
are discriminative enough to isolate the withheld attack family, while
Known-F1 measures the cost to closed-set performance of removing one class.

  see Table~\ref{tab:zeroday_wustl_both} in the appendix for the comparison.}
\label{tab:zeroday_summary}
\begin{tabular}{llccc}

\midrule

CIC-IoT2023  & Mirai-udpplain    & 31{,}864 & 0.8301 & 0.0004 \\
WUSTL-IIoT   & Reconn$^\dagger$  & 8{,}240  & \textbf{0.8005} & \textbf{0.9989} \\

The results span the full ZDR range. WUSTL-IIoT (Reconn) and TON\_IoT
(ransomware) achieve near-perfect ZDR ($\geq 0.999$) with only a modest
reduction in Known-F1, indicating that the withheld class occupies a distinct
region of feature space that the system can isolate without supervision. By
contrast, CIC-IoT2023 exhibits very low ZDR for both withheld classes
($\leq 0.022$), reflecting substantial overlap between withheld and known
traffic distributions. Bot-IoT achieves intermediate detection
(ZDR~$= 0.649$) at the cost of the highest Known-F1, suggesting that
Service\_Scan shares statistical signatures with known bot families.
UNSW-NB15 (Reconnaissance) yields a ZDR of $0.482$, the lowest of the
non-CIC scenarios, and is the only case where traditional ML classifiers
decisively outperform the LLM on zero-day detection
(DT: $0.845$, RF: $0.879$ versus LLM: $0.482$).
This reversal is consistent with the multiclass analysis. UNSW-NB15 Reconnaissance traffic shares volume-level statistical signatures with the known attack classes in the training set, so the threshold rules learned from known attacks fire on Reconnaissance flows at roughly chance rate. The anonymization ablation for this experiment confirms feature names are
irrelevant: $\Delta$ZDR~$= +0.005 \pm 0.009$ across three seeds, indicating
that the low ZDR is structural rather than name-driven.

For WUSTL-IIoT, two runs with different rule configurations were available;
Table~\ref{tab:zeroday_wustl_both} in Appendix~\ref{app:detailed} presents
both results (ZDR of 1.0 and 0.999 respectively).

Anonymization has not degraded detection performance across all five datasets. In three of five cases it actively improves it. CIC-IoT2023 ($\Delta\text{F1} = +0.071$) and TON\_IoT ($\Delta\text{F1} = +0.175$) both show a substantial positive shift under anonymization. For TON\_IoT, the absolute F1 gain of $+0.175$ is accompanied by an increase in seed-to-seed variance (std rises from $0.002$ to $0.052$ under anonymization), suggesting the LLM explores a wider range of rule configurations when feature names are opaque. WUSTL-IIoT converges to near-identical F1 under both conditions ($\Delta\text{F1} < 0.001$, Jaccard $= 0.369$), indicating that the two conditions discover different but equally discriminative feature subsets. Bot-IoT and UNSW-NB15 show near-neutral effects ($|\Delta\text{F1}| \leq 0.01$), consistent with datasets whose discriminative structure is axis-aligned and recoverable regardless of whether feature names carry semantic meaning. Across all five datasets, no case emerges in which semantic naming provides a statistically reliable advantage. The results suggest that the LLM policy generator relies primarily on numerical patterns in the representative samples rather than on feature-name semantics, a finding that has positive implications for deployment in environments where feature names may be obfuscated or non-descriptive.

\subsection{Adversarial Robustness under the Kerckhoffs Assumption}
\label{sec:kerckhoffs}
% ========================================================================

We evaluate evasion resilience under a Kerckhoffs-style threat model in which the attacker has full knowledge of the detection rules and applies the minimum feature perturbation (in standard-deviation units, $\varepsilon$) to evade detection. The summary metric is $\varepsilon$@ESR=0.5: the budget at which 50\% of flagged attacks are successfully evaded. We compare \texttt{LLM\textsubscript{orig}},
\texttt{LLM\textsubscript{anon}}, \texttt{DT\textsubscript{d=3}},
\texttt{DT\textsubscript{d=5}}, and \texttt{RF\textsubscript{d=5}} across
CIC-IoT2023 and WUSTL-IIoT, with results validated over three random seeds. ESR curves and feature targeting profiles are shown in Figs.~\ref{fig:robustness_curves} and~\ref{fig:feature_targeting}, respectively.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Median evasion cost ($\varepsilon$@ESR=0.5) under the Kerckhoffs threat
           model (mean over 3 seeds). Higher values indicate harder evasion.}
  \label{tab:kerckhoffs}
  \begin{tabular}{lccccc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Dataset} & \textbf{LLM\textsubscript{orig}} & \textbf{LLM\textsubscript{anon}} &
    \textbf{DT\textsubscript{d=3}} & \textbf{DT\textsubscript{d=5}} &
    \textbf{RF\textsubscript{d=5}} \\
    \midrule
    CIC-IoT2023 & 0.008 & 0.002 & 0.003 & 0.003 & 0.010 \\
    WUSTL-IIoT  & 0.002 & 0.018 & 0.035 & 0.001 & 0.001 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Results in Table~\ref{tab:kerckhoffs} show that \texttt{LLM\textsubscript{orig}} is comparable to \texttt{RF\textsubscript{d=5}} on CIC-IoT2023 ($\varepsilon$@0.5${=}0.008$ vs.\ $0.010$), demonstrating that LLM generated rules are not inherently more adversarially fragile than trained classifiers. Notably, \texttt{DT\textsubscript{d=5}} collapses to ESR${=}1.0$ at $\varepsilon{=}0.001$ on WUSTL-IIoT (70$\times$ more fragile than the shallower \texttt{DT\textsubscript{d=3}}) due to a single \textsc{IdleTime} bottleneck governing 99.97\% of detections. Critically, all conditions reach ESR${=}1.0$ at $\varepsilon \leq 0.05$, revealing a structural fragility shared by all conjunctive threshold based detectors, motivating adversarially-aware rule synthesis as future work.
